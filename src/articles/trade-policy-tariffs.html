Trade Policy Nobody Agrees What the Tariffs Did, Which Tells You Who They Worked For September 15, 2026

By Kade Rourke // Trois Cent 360

A tariff is a tax with a better publicist. Call it a wall against unfair competition and a price hike suddenly sounds like defense instead of extraction.

The White House's 2026 Trade Policy Agenda leans on the good version of the story: real disposable income up 1.6 percent in 2025, private-sector earnings climbing over $2,700 on average, the trade deficit finally getting treated as the emergency the administration's been calling it for years. The Federal Reserve's own regional reports tell a messier one — manufacturers in Philadelphia adjusting workforce and prices to absorb tariff costs, a Boston-area company laying people off specifically to offset them, inventories thinning out in ways that usually mean price hikes are coming, not already priced in. One independent estimate puts the average household's added cost in the four figures over the past year. Economists modeling the labor-market side project unemployment ticking up before any of it settles back to baseline, later this decade.

Both things are true at once, and that should bother you more than either one alone would. A trade policy doesn't have to be a lie to also not be free. Somebody's paying for the protection. It's rarely the corp writing the press release.
