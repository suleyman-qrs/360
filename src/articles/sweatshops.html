Sweatshops Silenced: What an Audit Is Actually For September 10, 2026

I used to think an audit was supposed to catch the bad thing. Turns out most of the time it's built to catch the complaint about the bad thing, and stop right there.

A report out of London this year, based on testimony from 255 garment workers across eight factories in Pakistan, lays out exactly how that works. Global fashion brands — Levi's, Next, H&M, Gap, Carrefour, Primark among them — pay into a billion-dollar compliance system that's supposed to catch unpaid wages, harassment, and unsafe conditions. Workers describe complaints going in and nothing coming back out. The report's title is blunt about it: Silenced.

None of this is new, and it isn't rare either — labor groups tracking Bangladesh's export sector are calling 2026 a "stress test" year, with funding for independent worker-solidarity organizations drying up right as heat-related deaths and wage-theft complaints keep climbing. A fire in a Dhaka garment factory last October killed sixteen people. The investigation into what caused it is, as far as I can tell, still open.

An audit isn't there to fix the floor. It's there so a customer three thousand miles away can glance at a certification badge and never ask what actually happens on the line. The workers know exactly what kind of visit they're getting when the inspector shows up. They just don't get a say in which kind it turns out to be.
