Biohazard Agrichem They Didn't Win in the Lab. They Won in a Courtroom. September 15, 2026

By Kade Rourke // Trois Cent 360

I've watched a lot of companies get away with things by outspending the people they hurt. I hadn't watched one get away with it by outspending the truth itself until this summer, and almost nobody outside the litigation trade noticed.

Bayer, the pharma-and-agrichem giant that bought Monsanto in 2018 and inherited its weedkiller Roundup along with it, has spent two decades fighting the same question in American courtrooms: did the company know its product could cause cancer, and did it fail to warn people using it? Roundup's active ingredient, glyphosate, is the most heavily used herbicide on the planet — American farmers spray roughly 250 million pounds of it across cropland every year. The World Health Organization's cancer research arm classified it as "probably carcinogenic to humans" back in 2015. Bayer has spent north of $11 billion settling claims from people who used the product and later developed non-Hodgkin lymphoma and other cancers, and as of this spring nearly 4,000 cases were still pending in federal court alone, with thousands more in state systems.

That's the part you might already half-know. Here's the part that should actually make you angry: this June, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 7–2 in Monsanto v. Durnell that federal pesticide law can block those failure-to-warn lawsuits outright. The case reached the court after Bayer appealed a $1.25 million verdict awarded to John Durnell, a home gardener diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma after using Roundup. The majority's reasoning wasn't that nobody looked at the cancer risk — the opinion leans on the fact that the EPA has repeatedly reviewed glyphosate specifically for carcinogenicity and concluded it's not likely to cause cancer. The real fight is over whether that federal conclusion should be allowed to override what a state court jury decides after hearing the same evidence. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented, joined by Justice Neil Gorsuch, arguing the ruling improperly shuts state-level plaintiffs out of court on a question EPA's own process was never built to settle for every future case.

The timing isn't incidental. Earlier this year, the administration signed an executive order aimed at boosting glyphosate production, a move that reportedly caused friction within its own coalition, given how many people who voted for this administration did so partly on promises to crack down on exactly this kind of chemical. Language that would have shielded pesticide makers from failure-to-warn suits was also stripped from the House farm bill and kept out of the Senate's draft — Congress, at least on paper, didn't want to hand Bayer this win. The Supreme Court gave it to them anyway.

Bayer never had to prove Roundup is safe in a way that persuaded every jury that's heard a case. It only had to win an argument about which regulator's conclusion gets the final word. That's the move companies have gotten very good at: you don't need to win the science with everyone if you can get one federal finding treated as the only one that counts. Every case still working through the courts under the old theory is running on borrowed time now.
