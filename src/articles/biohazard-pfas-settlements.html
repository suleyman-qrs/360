Biohazard Agrichem The Chemical That Doesn't Break Down, and the Bill That Keeps Coming Due September 15, 2026

By Kade Rourke // Trois Cent 360

There's a category of chemical that chemists nicknamed "forever chemicals" because that's literally what they do. Once PFAS compounds get into soil, water, or a human bloodstream, they don't break down the way most pollutants eventually do. They just stay. The U.S. Geological Survey estimates they're now in a substantial share of American tap water, with EPA identifying contamination in systems serving more than 110 million people.

The companies that made this stuff allegedly knew what it did for decades before the public found out — that's the claim at the center of tens of thousands of lawsuits now working through the courts, and it's the reason the settlement numbers involved have gotten genuinely enormous. 3M agreed to pay up to $12.5 billion over thirteen years in a nationwide settlement to help public water systems remove PFAS from their supply. Separately, in New Jersey specifically, a federal judge this August approved two more settlements: DuPont and related entities (Chemours and Corteva, both DuPont spinoffs) will pay about $2 billion — on its own the largest environmental settlement any single U.S. state has ever reached — and 3M will pay an additional $400 to $450 million on top of that, bringing the combined New Jersey package to roughly $2.5 billion. This June, Chemours also agreed to a separate $450 million settlement — the first comprehensive federal settlement against a PFAS manufacturer — covering releases in West Virginia, North Carolina, and New Jersey. As of this fall, there are still more than 15,000 PFAS-related lawsuits moving through a single federal multidistrict litigation process.

Here's the part that actually made me want to write this piece instead of just filing it under "yet another corporate settlement." When North Carolina's governor and attorney general reacted to the Chemours federal deal, they didn't sound like people celebrating a win — they sounded furious. The state's attorney general said the settlement did almost nothing to actually clean up the water in the Cape Fear River, where a PFAS compound called GenX has been detected for years, and put Chemours on notice that his office wasn't finished with them. That's what accountability looks like when the number attached to it is still smaller than the damage: everyone signs the paperwork, and the people closest to the contamination are the ones saying it isn't enough.

Meanwhile, the regulatory floor underneath all of this has been getting lower, not higher. The EPA proposed rolling back key parts of the national PFAS drinking water standard, the same standard that had only been finalized in 2024 after decades of advocacy. So the timeline, in order, runs: companies allegedly knew, companies didn't disclose, contamination spread into water systems serving over a hundred million Americans, the companies are now paying billions to partially clean up a mess measured in decades, and the federal rule meant to prevent the next version of this same story is currently being loosened rather than tightened.

A chemical that never breaks down doesn't need a company to keep polluting for the damage to keep growing. It just needs everyone downstream to keep drinking the water while the lawyers argue about how much of the bill gets paid, and when.
