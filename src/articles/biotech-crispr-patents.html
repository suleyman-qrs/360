Biotech Whoever Owns the Patent Owns the Cure's Price Tag September 15, 2026

By Kade Rourke // Trois Cent 360

CRISPR gene editing is fourteen years old, and the two institutions that arguably invented it are still suing each other over who gets to say so. In March 2026, the Patent Trial and Appeal Board ruled — again, on remand from the Federal Circuit — that the Broad Institute got to eukaryotic-cell gene editing first, ahead of the University of California/Berkeley team. Appeals are still open.

That fight isn't academic trivia. It's licensing leverage. Every company building a gene-editing therapy has to navigate a patent thicket older than their own research, and the legal uncertainty has a real cost downstream: several sickle-cell disease programs were reportedly shelved rather than raced to market against an already-approved competitor, because taking on an entrenched, well-licensed platform wasn't worth the risk to a smaller developer's balance sheet.

So the technology that could, in theory, edit out a genetic disease exists. What decides who actually gets access to it isn't the biology. It's freedom to operate — a phrase from patent law that describes precisely what it sounds like it describes, once you sit with it for a second.
