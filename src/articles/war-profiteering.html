War Profiteering The Backlog Doesn't Care Who Wins September 15, 2026

By Kade Rourke // Trois Cent 360

Every earnings call has a moment where the mask slips a little. This spring it was buried in an otherwise routine line from Lockheed Martin: their aeronautics unit took a hit from F-16 flight-test delays and supply-chain strain on the C-130 line, but rising F-35 sales made up the difference. Read that back. A weapons-production delay got offset by stronger weapons sales. That's the whole quarter, in one sentence, and nobody on the call blinked.

I want to be precise about what I'm accusing these companies of, because it's easy to make this sound like a cartoon and harder — more useful, too — to look at the actual mechanics. Nobody at Lockheed or Northrop Grumman or RTX has to want a war for this system to work exactly as built. They just need contracts that pay out more the longer a conflict runs, and let the accounting handle the rest.

The numbers this year make the shape of it hard to miss. Lockheed raised its 2026 guidance to full-year profit of $29.95 to $30.65 a share on $79.75 to $81.75 billion in revenue, citing sustained demand tied to "rising geopolitical uncertainty" — their phrase, and I think they undersold it. The company's own backlog hit a record $230 billion in Q2 2026 on $65 billion of new orders in the quarter alone. Lockheed also locked in a seven-year deal with the Department of War to more than triple Patriot missile interceptor production, from 600 units a year to 2,000. Northrop Grumman, meanwhile, is running a smaller but still enormous number: a backlog of roughly $96 to $105 billion through mid-2026, built in part on a deal with the Air Force to expand B-21 stealth bomber production by 25 percent, backed by about $2.5 billion of Northrop's own investment. Global defense spending crossed $2.63 trillion this year, a record.

Here's the part that should worry you more than the profit margins do. Precision missile interceptor stockpiles reportedly dropped by as much as a quarter during recent fighting between Israel and Iran, and the U.S. has burned through more than 50,000 rockets and missiles since Russia invaded Ukraine. Industry-funded analysts are now warning that a shrinking industrial base threatens military readiness — a real concern, sure, but also, conveniently, the exact argument that justifies handing more money to expand capacity to the same handful of companies already profiting from the shortage.

I used to think the ugly part of the war economy was the profiteering itself — some exec cashing a bonus while people somewhere are dying. I've changed my mind. The ugly part is how ordinary it sounds on an earnings call: forecasts, guidance, backlog, delivered in the flat procedural tone of quarterly reporting, as if the war were a supply-chain issue rather than the reason the supply chain exists in the first place.
