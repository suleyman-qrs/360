Cold Cases / Archives The File That Says Closed and the Family That Won't Let It Be September 15, 2026

By Kade Rourke // Trois Cent 360

A note before this one: it's about a real person who died less than two years ago, and his family is still grieving in public. I'm not writing this to settle anything. I'm writing it because the gap between what an institution calls "closed" and what a family calls "unanswered" is exactly the kind of story this section exists for.

Suchir Balaji was 26. He'd worked at OpenAI for four years, helping build the datasets that trained GPT-4, and he left the company in August 2024 after deciding, in his own words reported at the time, that the way those models were trained probably violated copyright law at a scale nobody wanted to talk about. He went public about it. Two months later, on November 26, 2024, he was found dead in his San Francisco apartment.

The San Francisco Police Department and the medical examiner's office investigated and, in a joint report released February 14–15, 2025, ruled it a suicide. No forced entry, no signs of a struggle, Balaji's own DNA on the gun. That's the closed file.

His parents don't accept it, and they haven't quietly disagreed from the sidelines — they've built an entire public campaign around it, hired their own investigators, and sued the police to force release of the full report. His mother has said she believes there was foul play, "from many factors, many data points," and has pointed to specific things she disputes about the scene and the official account. Neither parent has produced evidence that's changed the official finding. Both have kept pushing anyway.

What makes this one strange, rather than just sad, is who showed up to listen. Democratic Congressman Ro Khanna publicly called for a fuller investigation. Balaji's mother later sat for a lengthy interview with Tucker Carlson, laying out the family's case to an audience about as far from Khanna's as you can get in American politics. For a few weeks last year, a Bay Area whistleblower's death was one of the only things a Democratic congressman and a right-wing media host both seemed to agree deserved more scrutiny.

I don't know what happened in that apartment, and neither does anyone reading this. What I do know is that Balaji died a few months after telling reporters he thought the company he'd just left was doing something legally indefensible, and that the case against him staying quiet is stronger than the case for it. That pattern doesn't prove murder. It's exactly why "died by suicide, case closed" doesn't automatically end a story for the people who knew him, and why an archive like this one exists: not to solve it, but to make sure it doesn't quietly stop being asked about.
